      Language models improve when they can learn from each attempt, not just from a final score. Most current approaches look at whether a single rollout passes a verifier and then adjust the policy based on that binary outcome. This discards the step‑by‑step traces that show how the model arrived at an answer, even when the answer is wrong. The new work introduces a way to keep those traces, turn them into reusable procedural knowledge, and feed that knowledge back into the model during training.

      What You Need to Know

      The authors call their method Procedural Memory Distillation (PMD). During each training episode the model generates a rollout, and a verifier checks if the final answer is correct. Instead of stopping there, PMD records the entire sequence of actions and intermediate states that led to the outcome. Over many episodes it builds a memory of which action patterns repeatedly lead to success and which repeatedly lead to failure, regardless of the particular problem instance.

      That memory is then distilled: the frequent successful patterns are turned into soft targets for the policy, while the recurring failure patterns are used to steer the policy away from those behaviors. The distillation step updates the model’s weights directly, so the learned procedural knowledge becomes part of the model’s internal representation rather than staying in an external buffer.

      Because the memory aggregates signals across episodes and across policy updates, it captures regularities that episode‑local reinforcement learning updates miss. The method works on top of existing RLVR or SDPO pipelines, requiring only a logger for the rollout traces and a simple clustering or counting mechanism to extract the reusable patterns.

      Why It Matters

      Current self‑improving loops often stall when the model keeps making the same mistake on slightly different problems. By explicitly remembering which procedural steps cause those mistakes, PMD can reduce redundant errors and accelerate convergence. Experiments in the paper show measurable gains on math and code generation benchmarks where verifiable rewards are available.

      Beyond performance, the approach offers a clearer view of what the model has learned. The distilled procedural memory can be inspected to see which strategies the model favors, providing a useful diagnostic tool for researchers who want to understand failure modes without relying solely on final accuracy scores.

      Key Details

      • Online collection: Each rollout’s action‑state trace is stored as it is generated.
      • Pattern extraction: Simple frequency counting or clustering identifies action sequences that appear above a threshold in successful versus failed rollouts.
      • Distillation loss: Successful patterns are added as target log‑probabilities; failed patterns are penalized via a negative‑likelihood term.
      • Weight update: The loss is back‑propagated through the policy network, merging procedural knowledge into the model’s parameters.
      • Compatibility: PMD plugs into existing RLVR or SDPO pipelines without changing the reward verifier.
      • Scalability: Memory size is kept bounded by discarding low‑frequency patterns, allowing the method to run on large‑scale language model training.

      What's Next

      The authors suggest extending PMD to environments where verifiers are noisy or where the reward signal is sparse, using uncertainty estimates to weight the extracted patterns. They also propose exploring hierarchical procedural memories that capture sub‑routines across different task families, which could further improve generalization and reduce the need for massive amounts of rollout data.
