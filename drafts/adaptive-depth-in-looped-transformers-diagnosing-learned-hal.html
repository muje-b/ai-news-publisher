Adaptive Depth in Looped Transformers: Diagnosing Learned Halting Gates and Trajectory Readouts

When a transformer reuses the same block many times, the model can decide when to stop computing. Looped Transformers implement this idea by repeatedly applying a shared recurrent block and learning a “halting gate” that tells the network whether to continue or exit. The gate is trained with a single distribution that serves two purposes: it determines when inference should stop, and it weights the loss contributed by each intermediate depth. Because the same signal drives both stopping and supervision, improvements in adaptive compute can be hindered by problems in the gate, the trajectory of hidden states, or the way the two interact.

What You Need to Know

The paper introduces a trajectory–readout framework to disentangle these factors. First, they isolate the halting gate by fixing the recurrent dynamics and observing how changes in the exit distribution affect stopping behavior alone. Second, they freeze the gate and examine the sequence of hidden states (the trajectory) produced for each input, measuring how well those states solve the task without any further weighting. Finally, they recombine the two to see whether poor performance stems from a mis‑calibrated gate, a sub‑optimal trajectory, or a mismatch between the two.

Experiments focus on two synthetic reasoning tasks that require variable numbers of steps: modular addition (e.g., computing (a+b) mod N) and binary parity (determining whether the number of 1’s in a bit string is odd or even). Both tasks admit a clear curriculum of depth: easy instances finish in few steps, hard instances need many recursions. By controlling the difficulty, the authors can see whether the looped transformer learns to allocate more computation where it is truly needed.

The results show that the learned halting gate often correlates with task difficulty, but the correlation is noisy. In many cases the gate exits early on hard examples, forcing the network to rely on an under‑computed trajectory. Conversely, on easy examples the gate sometimes lingers, wasting compute on states that add little predictive value. The trajectory analysis reveals that the recurrent block itself can produce correct answers after a modest number of steps, indicating that the bottleneck is often the gate’s weighting rather than the representation quality.

Why It Matters

Understanding the separation between stopping rules and representation learning is crucial for building models that dynamically allocate computation without sacrificing accuracy. If the halting signal conflates these roles, engineers may spend effort tweaking the gate when the real issue lies in the recurrent dynamics, or vice‑versa. By providing diagnostic tools, the paper helps practitioners pinpoint whether to improve the gating mechanism, redesign the recurrent block, or adjust the loss weighting scheme.

Beyond looped transformers, the insights apply to any adaptive‑compute architecture—such as adaptive attention spans, dynamic depth networks, or early‑exit classifiers—where a single scalar governs both inference timing and training supervision. Clearer diagnostics can lead to more stable training curves, better compute‑accuracy trade‑offs, and simpler hyper‑parameter selection for real‑world deployment where latency budgets are strict.

Key Details

  • Task suite: modular addition (varying modulus) and binary parity (varying length) to create a graded difficulty curve.
  • Halting gate isolation: freeze recurrent weights, train only the exit distribution; measure stopping accuracy independent of trajectory quality.
  • Trajectory evaluation: freeze the gate, unroll the recurrent block for a fixed number of steps, and compute task performance at each depth to see where the representation becomes sufficient.
  • Joint analysis: recombine gate and trajectory to quantify performance loss due to early exit, late exit, or mis‑weighted intermediate losses.
  • Findings: the recurrent block often reaches sufficient accuracy after fewer steps than the gate indicates; gate mis‑calibration accounts for a large share of compute inefficiency.
  • Implications: suggests alternative training objectives—such as separate losses for halting and representation—or auxiliary supervision to better align gate decisions with useful trajectory states.

What's Next

Future work could explore decoupled loss functions that train the halt signal purely on a validation‑based stopping criterion while using a distinct signal to supervise the recurrent states. Another direction is to inject noise or curriculum‑based gating during training to encourage the gate to wait until the trajectory has truly matured. Extending these diagnostics to natural language tasks—where the notion of “necessary steps” is less explicit—will test whether the trajectory–readout lens scales beyond synthetic arithmetic and parity benchmarks.
