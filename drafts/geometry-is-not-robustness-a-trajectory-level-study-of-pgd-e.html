          Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) remains the de‑facto benchmark for testing how well a neural network withstands adversarial perturbations. Most studies stop at the final success rate after a fixed number of steps, which hides what happens during the attack itself. A new pre‑print looks inside the attack trajectory to ask whether the intermediate behaviour can tell us anything reliable about a model’s true robustness.

          What You Need to Know

          The authors train several convolutional neural networks on Fashion‑MNIST, comparing models obtained with standard empirical risk minimization to those produced by PGD‑based adversarial training. All networks share the same architecture: two 3×3 convolutional layers with ReLU, max‑pooling, and a small fully‑connected head. For each model they run 20‑step PGD attacks with step size set to ε/4 (ε=0.3 in ℓ∞ norm), using a random uniform start inside the allowed perturbation ball and repeating each attack five times with different seeds.

          During the attack they record three trajectory‑level diagnostics at every iteration: the classification loss, the cosine similarity between the gradient of the loss w.r.t. the input and the current perturbation direction (gradient alignment), and the step at which the loss surpasses a pre‑defined failure threshold (steps‑to‑failure). These metrics are averaged over the restarts and over the test set to obtain a per‑model trajectory profile.

          Why It Matters

          Relying only on the final adversarial accuracy can mask important differences in how models respond to an ongoing attack. If two classifiers have similar robust accuracy but one loses confidence early while the other resists longer, the latter may be preferable in settings where detection or early‑stopping is possible. Understanding the shape of the loss curve or the decay of gradient alignment helps designers spot weaknesses that a single number would miss.

          At the same time, the study shows that no single trajectory signal reliably predicts robustness across the examined regimes. This cautions against replacing the standard metric with any one diagnostic and encourages the community to look for richer, possibly multi‑dimensional, ways to characterise adversarial optimisation dynamics.

          Key Details

          • Dataset: Fashion‑MNIST (10 classes, 28×28 grayscale images).
          • Model architecture: two conv layers (3×3, 32 and 64 filters) + ReLU + max‑pool + fully‑connected layer (128 units) → softmax.
          • Training regimes: standard ERM, PGD adversarial training with ε=0.3 for 5, 10, and 20 epochs.
          • Attack configuration: 20‑step PGD, step size α=ε/4, random uniform init in ε‑ball, 5 restarts per image.
          • Diagnostics per step: loss value, gradient‑alignment cosine similarity, steps‑to‑failure (first step where loss > 0.5·initial loss).
