Towards On-Board Implementation of ML-Based Helicopter Weight Estimator

Estimating a helicopter’s weight accurately at takeoff is a routine yet critical task for flight safety and performance. Traditionally, pilots rely on manual calculations or simple lookup tables that can be prone to error, especially when aircraft configurations change frequently. A recent arXiv pre‑print describes how a supervised machine‑learning approach, trained on extensive in‑service data from Airbus helicopters, can be embedded directly into legacy avionics to provide a more reliable weight estimate in real time.

What You Need to Know

The paper presents a supervised learning pipeline that takes sensor readings available during the takeoff roll—such as engine torque, rotor speed, and environmental conditions—and outputs an estimate of the helicopter’s gross weight. The model is a long short‑term memory (LSTM) recurrent neural network, chosen for its ability to capture temporal dependencies in the short sequence of measurements leading up to lift‑off. Training data were drawn from Airbus’s global fleet, covering a wide range of models, configurations, and operating conditions, which helps the network learn generalized patterns rather than over‑fitting to a specific aircraft.

To satisfy aviation certification concerns, the authors followed a learning assurance process that mirrors the EASA concept paper for machine‑learning applications and aligns with the ongoing Eurocae ED‑324 standard. They defined a concrete set of machine‑learning requirements (e.g., input data bounds, output accuracy thresholds, latency limits) and documented the model architecture, training procedure, and verification steps. The implementation was tested on a representative legacy avionics computer, demonstrating that the LSTM inference fits within the processing budget and memory constraints typical of existing flight‑control units.

Why It Matters

Accurate weight estimation directly influences takeoff performance calculations, including required runway length, climb gradient, and fuel planning. Errors in weight can lead to either unnecessary performance margins (increasing operating costs) or, worse, insufficient safety margins. By providing a data‑driven estimate that updates continuously as the aircraft accelerates, the ML‑based estimator offers a way to reduce reliance on static tables and manual pilot input, potentially improving both safety and efficiency.

Moreover, the work shows that modern ML techniques can be adapted to the stringent constraints of airborne hardware without requiring a full avionics retrofit. Demonstrating feasibility on legacy systems lowers the barrier for adoption across existing fleets, allowing operators to benefit from improved weight awareness while awaiting newer aircraft generations that might incorporate similar capabilities natively.

Key Details

  • Model type: LSTM recurrent neural network with a single hidden layer.
  • Input features: engine torque, rotor RPM, ambient temperature, pressure, and control surface positions sampled at 10 Hz.
  • Output: predicted helicopter gross weight in kilograms, updated every second during the takeoff roll.
  • Performance target: mean absolute error ≤ 50 kg and inference latency ≤ 20 ms on the test avionics board.
  • Verification: requirements checked via unit tests, simulation against flight‑test data, and robustness checks for sensor noise and missing data.
  • Hardware demonstration: executed successfully on a legacy PowerPC‑based flight‑computer similar to those used in current Airbus H‑series helicopters.

What's Next

The authors suggest extending the approach to other flight phases, such as landing weight estimation, and exploring ensemble or hybrid models that combine physics‑based estimates with ML corrections. Further work will focus on formal certification evidence generation, including documentation of data provenance, model monitoring strategies, and plans for periodic re‑training as fleet data evolves. These steps aim to move the estimator from a proof‑of‑concept on a test bench to a qualified component that can be deployed in operational helicopters.
