Beyond the Hivemind: Escaping LLM Homogeneity via Meta-Persona Anchoring and Sequential Temperature Scaling

The idea that large language models sometimes sound like a single voice has become a recurring observation among researchers and users alike. When you ask an LLM an open‑ended question, the answers often cluster tightly around a few similar phrasing patterns, even when you crank up the temperature setting meant to encourage randomness. This tendency, dubbed the “Artificial Hivemind” effect, limits the usefulness of models for tasks that benefit from varied perspectives, such as brainstorming, creative writing, or exploring alternative hypotheses. A new paper tackles this problem by giving the model a way to start from a distinct point of view and then guiding its sampling process to preserve that individuality.

What You Need to Know

The authors introduce a two‑stage method called Meta‑Persona Anchoring combined with Filtered Temperature Scaling (FTS). In the first stage, the model is prompted to generate a short, self‑selected description of a persona—think of a brief character sketch that includes quirks, background, or a particular stance. This persona acts as an anchor, steering the model’s internal representation away from the default consensus. In the second stage, the model produces the final answer while a dual‑stage sampling sieve is applied. First, a Top‑p filter removes extremely unlikely tokens, preserving grammatical fluency. Then, a temperature scaling step adjusts the probability distribution in a way that rewards tokens aligned with the chosen persona while still allowing sufficient randomness to avoid repetition.

Empirical tests show that this approach reduces inter‑response similarity from the typical 0.80‑0.90 range down to about 0.55‑0.65 on a set of open‑ended prompts, without a noticeable drop in coherence or factual accuracy. The improvement holds across several model sizes and families, suggesting the technique is not tightly coupled to a particular architecture.

Why It Matters

When language models converge on a narrow consensus, they miss opportunities to surface diverse viewpoints that could be valuable in collaborative settings. For example, a design team using an LLM to generate concepts might receive many similar ideas, limiting the exploration of the solution space. By encouraging the model to adopt distinct personas, the output spreads across a broader semantic area, increasing the chance of uncovering unconventional or novel suggestions.

Beyond creativity, the method also aids applications that require balanced representation, such as simulating stakeholder opinions in policy analysis or generating varied customer‑service responses. Reducing homogenization makes the model’s behavior more predictable in the sense that users can anticipate a spread of answers rather than a single dominant pattern, which can improve trust and usability in systems where variability is a feature rather than a bug.

Key Details

  • Meta‑Persona Anchoring: model generates a self‑described persona before answering.
  • Filtered Temperature Scaling: Top‑p filtering followed by temperature adjustment that biases toward the anchored persona.
  • Two‑stage generation: persona creation → answer production with the sampling sieve.
  • Evaluation metric: average pairwise cosine similarity of responses (target <0.65).
  • Tested on models ranging from 125M to 13B parameters across multiple families.
  • No significant degradation in perplexity or factuality scores on benchmark tasks.

What's Next

Future work could explore how to automatically calibrate the strength of persona anchoring based on task requirements, or how to combine this approach with external knowledge retrieval to keep diversity from compromising correctness. Additionally, investigating the interaction between multiple anchored personas in a single dialogue could open paths toward more dynamic, multi‑view conversational agents.
