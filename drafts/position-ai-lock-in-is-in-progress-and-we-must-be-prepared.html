Position: AI Lock-In Is in Progress, and We Must Be Prepared

Artificial intelligence systems are becoming part of daily work, education, and personal life. As reliance grows, researchers warn that we may be sacrificing skills and resilience that keep societies functioning when technology falters. This post summarizes a position paper that calls for safety research to expand its focus to a neglected risk: AI Lock‑In.

What You Need to Know

The paper defines AI Lock‑In as the process whereby heavy dependence on AI erodes human ability to perform tasks without machine assistance. Over time, people lose practice in critical thinking, manual problem‑solving, and interpersonal coordination that AI currently handles. The authors argue this deskilling is not a distant scenario but an observable trend in sectors such as customer service, medical diagnostics, and software development.

They note that AI safety research has concentrated on two areas: making AI outputs align with human values (technical alignment) and regulating the societal effects of generative models (job loss, misinformation). While important, these efforts ignore the systemic vulnerability that emerges when humans outsource core competencies to machines and then face disruptions—whether due to technical failure, cyber‑attack, or policy restrictions.

Why It Matters

When AI becomes unavailable, organizations that have deskilled their workforce may experience prolonged downtime, costly retraining, or even safety hazards. For example, a hospital that relies on AI‑driven imaging analysis could struggle to interpret scans if the system is compromised, putting patient care at risk. The societal impact extends beyond economics; democratic deliberation, craftsmanship, and community resilience all benefit from citizens who can act independently of algorithms.

Addressing AI Lock‑In is therefore a proactive safety measure. By preserving and cultivating human capabilities alongside AI deployment, societies can build redundancy that mitigates the consequences of any single point of failure. The paper urges policymakers, industry leaders, and researchers to treat skill maintenance as a core component of AI risk management.

Key Details

  • The paper reviews empirical studies showing reduced manual proficiency in fields using decision‑support AI.
  • It highlights case studies where AI outages led to operational pauses due to lack of human fallback.
  • Authors propose a framework for measuring deskilling through skill‑use audits and competency assessments.
  • Recommendations include designing AI systems that encourage human‑in‑the‑loop oversight rather than full substitution.
  • The position calls for funding programs that support continuous skill development alongside AI adoption.

What's Next

Future work should translate the concept of AI Lock‑In into concrete metrics that can be monitored across industries. Researchers are encouraged to pilot interventions—such as periodic skill‑refresh workshops or AI‑triggered “manual mode” exercises—and evaluate their effectiveness in maintaining human readiness. Integrating these practices into AI governance standards will help ensure that technological dependence does not come at the cost of collective resilience.
