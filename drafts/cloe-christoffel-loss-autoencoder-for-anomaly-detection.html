CLOE: Christoffel Loss Autoencoder for Anomaly Detection

Anomaly detection is a routine task in many industrial and scientific settings where only normal operating data are available for training. Semi‑supervised approaches that learn a model of normality and then flag deviations are attractive because they avoid the need for labeled anomalies. However, many existing techniques either become computationally heavy in high dimensions or require delicate tuning of several hyperparameters, which limits their usability in practice.

What You Need to Know

The paper introduces CLOE, which couples a standard autoencoder with a Christoffel‑function based scoring mechanism. The autoencoder first maps the input data to a lower‑dimensional latent space, reducing the curse of dimensionality that hinders direct Christoffel evaluations. In the latent space, the method computes the empirical Christoffel function, which serves as a density‑like score; low scores indicate points that lie far from the learned normal manifold and are therefore flagged as anomalies.

A key feature of CLOE is that, after the autoencoder is trained, the Christoffel step introduces at most one additional hyperparameter—the regularization term that controls the width of the function. The autoencoder itself is trained with a reconstruction loss, and the overall objective can be optimized end‑to‑end or in a two‑stage fashion, depending on computational constraints. The authors provide pseudocode and discuss how to select the latent dimensionality based on explained variance.

Experimental results are reported on several benchmark datasets, including high‑dimensional image collections (e.g., MNIST Fashion, CIFAR‑10) and real‑world process monitoring streams. CLOE consistently achieves AUROC scores comparable to or better than recent deep‑baseline methods while using far fewer tunable parameters and requiring less training time than comparable deep‑only approaches.

Why It Matters

Many anomaly‑detection pipelines in industry operate under strict latency and maintenance constraints. Methods that need extensive hyperparameter searches increase operational cost and risk of sub‑optimal deployment. By limiting the tuning burden to a single scalar, CLOE offers a practical alternative that can be deployed with minimal expert intervention, especially when data dimensionality is large and labeling anomalies is infeasible.
