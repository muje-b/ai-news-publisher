From Doyle to AGM: A Survey and an Implementation Roadmap for Belief Change

Belief change sits at the intersection of logic, artificial intelligence, and knowledge representation. When agents receive new information, they must decide how to adjust their existing beliefs without falling into inconsistency. This paper offers a focused narrative review that maps the historical trajectory of computational belief change, beginning with Doyle and London’s 1980 taxonomy and moving through the development of the AGM framework to present‑day approaches. By tracing how early pragmatic methods relate to later theoretical constructs, the authors illuminate continuities and shifts that shape today’s implementation challenges.

What You Need to Know

The review starts with Doyle and London’s classification of belief revision operators, which distinguished between contraction, expansion, and revision based on syntactic and semantic considerations. These early categories were motivated by the needs of rule‑based systems and non‑monotonic reasoning engines. The authors show how each operator was originally implemented using simple algorithms such as truth‑maintenance systems or priority‑based default hierarchies.

Next, the paper surveys the AGM (Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, Makinson) postulates, which formalized rationality constraints on belief change. It explains how the AGM framework transformed the informal taxonomy into a set of eight postulates (success, inclusion, vacuity, consistency, extensionality, superextensionality, recovery, and relevance) and corresponding representation theorems. The review highlights the gap between the idealized AGM assumptions—such as logical omniscience—and the computational realities faced by early systems.

Finally, the authors trace the post‑AGM era, identifying three major strands: (1) probabilistic belief revision, (2) iterated revision operators, and (3) resource‑bounded approximation methods. For each strand, they note the theoretical foundations that carry over from Doyle and London or AGM, and they point out where new assumptions (e.g., ranking functions, distance metrics) were introduced to handle dynamics and scalability.

Why It Matters

Understanding the lineage of belief change operators helps developers choose appropriate techniques for specific applications. For instance, a diagnostic system that requires quick, incremental updates may benefit from iterated revision operators, whereas a legal reasoning tool that demands strict logical consistency might rely on AGM‑inspired contraction. The review makes these trade‑offs explicit by linking historical motivations to modern performance characteristics.

Moreover, the paper identifies persistent implementation challenges—such as handling inconsistent inputs, managing computational complexity, and integrating belief change with learning mechanisms—that have persisted since the 1980s. By making these challenges visible, the work provides a roadmap for researchers aiming to bridge the gap between theoretical postulates and practical algorithms.

Key Details

  • The paper surveys over 150 references spanning four decades of belief change research.
  • It maps each of Doyle and London’s three operator classes to specific AGM postulates and their computational counterparts.
  • It details how truth‑maintenance systems correspond to the AGM recovery postulate in limited contexts.
  • It outlines three approximation strategies: bounded‑depth revision, sampling‑based belief states, and tractable Horn‑fragment restrictions.
  • It notes that probabilistic revision often relaxes the AGM consistency postulate in favor of coherence measures.
  • The authors propose a checklist for evaluating new belief change algorithms against historical criteria.

What's Next

The review concludes by suggesting that future work should hybridize AGM‑style logical guarantees with probabilistic uncertainty handling, while respecting real‑time resource limits. It calls for standardized benchmark suites that capture both static consistency checks and dynamic revision sequences, enabling clearer comparison across the diverse methods outlined in the survey.
