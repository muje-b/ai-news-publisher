Artists are lawyering up against AI slop, and some are even winning

When The Atlantic released a searchable list of the texts and images used to train today’s generative‑AI models, many creators typed their names into the box out of simple curiosity. Kirk Wallace Johnson, author of The Feather Thief and The Fishermen and the Dragon, did the same and saw his books appear in the data. His discovery sparked a broader reaction: artists, writers, and illustrators are now consulting lawyers and filing claims that their work was copied without permission to build AI systems.

What You Need to Know

The dataset published by The Atlantic pulls from a variety of public‑domain and copyrighted sources that were ingested by companies developing large language models and image generators. Johnson found that both of his nonfiction titles were listed among the thousands of works used to train these models. He contacted intellectual‑property counsel, who confirmed that the inclusion likely violates his copyright unless a license or fair‑use defense applies.

Johnson is not alone. A growing coalition of creators—including illustrators, photographers, and musicians—has begun sending cease‑and‑desist letters and filing lawsuits against AI firms. Some of these actions have already produced results, such as court orders requiring companies to remove specific works from their training sets or to negotiate licensing agreements.

Why It Matters

The outcome of these disputes will shape how AI developers source training material. If courts consistently rule that unlicensed use of copyrighted text or images constitutes infringement, companies may need to overhaul their data‑gathering practices, seek licenses, or rely more heavily on truly public‑domain or synthetic data.

For artists, the stakes are financial and moral. Unauthorized use can diminish the market for original works and erode the incentive to create new content. A legal framework that respects creators’ rights could ensure that AI innovation does not come at the expense of the very people whose work fuels the technology.

Key Details

  • Kirk Wallace Johnson discovered his books The Feather Thief (2018) and The Fishermen and the Dragon (2020) in The Atlantic’s searchable AI‑training dataset.
  • The dataset includes tens of thousands of copyrighted works that were scraped without explicit permission from rights holders.
  • Johnson consulted copyright attorneys who concluded that the use likely infringes his exclusive rights unless a license or fair‑use argument applies.
  • Similar claims have been filed by visual artists against image‑generation models, resulting in preliminary injunctions that forced temporary removal of disputed works from training data.
  • Some defendants have entered settlement talks, agreeing to pay licensing fees or to exclude specific creators’ works from future model updates.
  • Legal experts warn that a wave of litigation could prompt AI companies to adopt transparent data‑sourcing policies and opt‑out mechanisms for creators.

What's Next

As more creators review the dataset and assert their rights, we can expect an increase in filings, potential class‑action suits, and possibly legislative clarification on the scope of fair use for AI training. The coming months will test whether the current AI development model can coexist with existing copyright protections.
